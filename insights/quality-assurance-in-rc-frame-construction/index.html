The critical role of quality assurance in RC frame construction

Cover, compaction and curing decide a reinforced-concrete frame’s durability long before any defect is visible. How independent QA holds the line on the records that matter at handover.

A
Dr Arasti Afrasiabi
April 2026

A reinforced-concrete frame is unforgiving: almost everything that governs its long-term durability is decided in the hours around each pour and then sealed permanently inside the structure. Quality assurance on an RC frame is therefore not paperwork — it is the only point at which those decisions can still be influenced.

In the UK, the framework for that assurance is well established. Execution of concrete structures is governed by BS EN 13670, and many projects use the National Structural Concrete Specification (NSCS) as the basis of their specification. Durability requirements — exposure classes, concrete quality and cover — come from BS 8500 and Eurocode 2. Independent QA checks the works against those requirements as they are built.

The three that decide durability

Three controls do most of the work in determining whether a frame lasts its design life: concrete cover to the reinforcement, compaction of the placed concrete, and curing in the days after the pour. Each is invisible in the finished structure, and each is where independent QA earns its place.

  • Cover protects the steel from carbonation and chloride ingress — too little and corrosion is only a matter of time.
  • Compaction removes the voids and honeycombing that compromise strength and durability.
  • Curing develops the strength and density the mix was designed to deliver.

Cover: minimum, nominal and tolerance

Cover is often discussed as a single figure, but it has two parts. Minimum cover is set by durability and bond requirements for the exposure class and design life, determined through BS 8500 and Eurocode 2. Nominal cover — the figure on the drawings and used to size spacers — adds an allowance for fixing deviation, denoted Δc,dev. In UK practice that allowance is commonly 10 mm, although the project specification may set a different value.

The practical consequence is that the fixing allowance is not spare margin. Nominal cover is the figure that, with normal fixing deviation, still achieves the minimum. Spacers smaller than nominal, or reinforcement displaced during the pour, erode that margin directly. After striking, covermeter checks on the hardened element give an independent indication of the cover actually achieved.

Concrete conformity

Concrete is specified and supplied in accordance with BS EN 206 and its UK complementary standard, BS 8500. Conformity is primarily the producer’s responsibility, but the site has a role: checking delivery tickets against the specified concrete, recording where each load was placed, and ensuring that sampling and cube testing are carried out where the specification requires.

Cube results arrive after the concrete is in place, which is why the records linking each set of cubes to a pour location matter. Without them, a low result cannot be traced to the element it represents.

Non-conforming results should trigger a defined process — investigation, assessment of the element concerned and, where necessary, further testing — rather than an informal discussion. The ITP should state in advance who is notified and what happens next.

Curing

BS EN 13670 sets requirements for curing and protection, including curing classes that relate the duration of curing to the development of surface strength; the project specification normally states which class applies. In practice, curing is the control most often compromised by programme pressure, and the one whose omission is least visible afterwards.

Good curing records note the method used — retained formwork, curing membranes, wet coverings or sheeting — when it was applied and how long it was maintained. Where the weather changes, the records should show how protection was adapted. Without that information, there is no way to demonstrate after the event that curing met the specification.

Hold-points and inspection and test plans

A structured inspection regime is normally organised through an inspection and test plan (ITP), listing each activity, the requirement it is checked against, the evidence to be recorded and who signs it off. Within the ITP, hold-points are the stages at which work may not proceed until inspection has taken place and been recorded.

  • Pre-pour: reinforcement type, size, spacing, laps and fixing; cover and spacers; formwork condition; cast-in items; cleanliness.
  • During the pour: concrete conformity at delivery, placing sequence, compaction and sampling.
  • After the pour: curing applied and maintained; protection; striking in line with the specified criteria.
  • After striking: surface condition, dimensional checks and covermeter checks of cover.

Hold-points only work if the notice period is agreed and respected. If work can proceed without inspection because the inspector was not given notice, the hold-point exists on paper only.

Why independence matters here

A contractor’s own QA confirms the contractor’s own work to the contractor’s own programme. Independent QA verifies the same work against the specification with no stake in passing it — and crucially, it produces records made for the asset owner, not the installer.

On a concealed structure, the inspection record is the asset. If it was not captured independently, it barely exists.

What good QA captures

The value of independent oversight is realised years later, when a question about the frame can be answered from contemporaneous evidence rather than recollection.

  • Verified cover and reinforcement fixing before concealment, with photographic evidence.
  • Pour records — placement, compaction, concrete conformity — held independently.
  • Documented hold-points with clear sign-off authority.
  • A traceable audit trail that survives scrutiny in negotiation or dispute.

Those records are most useful when organised by element and location rather than by date alone, so that anyone examining a column, wall or slab years later can find what was checked, when, by whom and against which requirement.

Records and the golden thread

For most buildings, independent records serve the asset owner: they support handover, inform maintenance and provide evidence if questions arise later. For higher-risk buildings, the Building Safety Act 2022 introduced requirements for accurate, up-to-date information about the building to be created and maintained — commonly referred to as the golden thread. The precise duties sit with the relevant dutyholders and depend on the building and the stage of the project, but well-structured, contemporaneous construction records are an important part of the information that regime relies on.

Key takeaways
  • RC frame durability is set in the hours around each pour and then concealed.
  • Cover, compaction and curing are the three controls that matter most.
  • Nominal cover includes a fixing allowance; spacers and fixing must protect it.
  • An ITP with defined hold-points turns inspection into evidence.
  • Independent, contemporaneous records are what hold up years later.

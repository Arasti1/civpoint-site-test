Delamination in concrete slabs: causes and prevention

Premature surface sealing traps bleed water and air beneath the trowelled crust. The finishing-window mechanics behind delamination — and the site controls that prevent it.

A
Dr Arasti Afrasiabi
May 2026

Delamination is the separation of a thin, dense trowelled layer from the concrete beneath it. It often goes unnoticed until the floor is trafficked, when hollow-sounding areas break away under load. Unlike a structural crack, delamination is almost always a finishing-process defect — which means it is almost always preventable.

It is also frequently misdiagnosed. Hollow areas, surface lifting and cracking can each have several causes, and a repair chosen on the wrong diagnosis rarely lasts. This article sets out the mechanism, the conditions that drive it, how it is detected and how it is prevented.

The mechanism

When the surface is closed by trowelling before bleed water and entrapped air have escaped, a sealed crust forms over a weak, moisture-rich plane. As that trapped water and air migrate, they collect beneath the crust and break the bond. The slab can test perfectly for strength and still delaminate, because the defect lives in the top few millimetres.

  • Early hard-trowelling seals the surface before bleed has finished.
  • Entrapped air beneath the sealed crust forms a debonded plane.
  • Air-entrained mixes and rapid drying conditions sharply raise the risk.

Air content and hard-trowelled floors

Air entrainment improves freeze–thaw resistance in external concrete, but it works against a hard-trowelled interior floor. Entrained air slows bleeding and makes the surface feel ready to finish before the concrete beneath has released its water and air — exactly the condition that produces delamination. Guidance on floor construction published by the American Concrete Institute advises that air-entrained concrete should not be used for hard-trowelled floors, and that total air content should not exceed 3%.

Air content is not only a matter of deliberate entrainment. Some admixtures and mix designs can raise it unintentionally, so it should be specified, checked where the specification requires, and reviewed if the finishing behaviour of the concrete changes during a pour.

The conditions that drive it

Delamination is a timing problem made worse by environment. Any factor that accelerates the set of the surface relative to the body of the slab widens the gap between a surface that looks ready and a slab that actually is.

  • A cold subgrade or subbase keeps the lower part of the slab cool and slows its set, while warmer air above stiffens the top first.
  • An impermeable subbase or vapour membrane directly beneath the slab forces all bleed water to rise through the top surface, prolonging the bleed period.
  • High wind, low humidity and direct sun accelerate surface drying and set.
  • Dry-shake toppings applied too early, too heavily or onto bleed water can seal the surface or create a weak layer that later separates.

Several of these conditions often coincide — in partially enclosed buildings early in the year, for example, or where a slab is placed directly on an insulated or membraned base. The finishing crew can see the surface; they cannot see the body of the slab. That is why the finishing window has to be read with care rather than assumed.

Delamination is rarely a concrete problem. It is a decision — made with a trowel, a few minutes too early.

Detecting delamination

Delaminated areas typically sound hollow when struck. Sounding — with a hammer on small areas or a chain drag across larger floors — is the usual first method of detection, allowing hollow zones to be marked and mapped. Where the extent or depth is uncertain, cores can confirm the plane of separation and its relationship to the finishing operations.

Not every hollow sound is delamination, and the distinction matters because the causes and remedies differ.

  • Delamination: a thin trowelled layer, usually a few millimetres thick, debonded from the concrete beneath, often in patches.
  • Curling: lifting of slab edges and corners through differential drying; a hollow sound near joints reflects loss of support beneath the slab, not a debonded surface layer.
  • Blistering: small, localised bumps that form during finishing through the same trapped air and water mechanism, on a smaller scale; they may later break open.

Remediation in principle

Repair of a delaminated area generally involves defining its extent by sounding, cutting out the debonded material back to sound concrete with a clean, defined edge, preparing the substrate, and reinstating with a repair material suited to the floor’s duty and finish. The right material and method depend on the depth and area affected, the traffic and the floor’s surface regularity requirements.

Repairs will usually differ in appearance from the surrounding floor, and their performance depends on the quality of preparation and on the cause having been correctly identified. Where the cause is not understood, the risk of further delamination in adjacent areas remains.

Before any repair is specified, it is worth recording the extent of delaminated areas on a floor plan, alongside pour records, weather conditions and finishing times where these are available. Patterns often point to the cause — delamination concentrated near doors, along one pour edge or over an insulated area tells a very different story from delamination scattered across a bay — and that understanding determines whether the problem is likely to recur.

Preventing it on site

Prevention is about reading the slab, not the clock. Independent QA/QC oversight at the pour focuses on the finishing window precisely because that is where the defect is created or avoided.

  • Do not begin final trowelling until bleeding has stopped and the surface can bear the operation.
  • Avoid air-entrained concrete for hard-trowelled interior floors unless specifically justified, and check air content.
  • Manage drying conditions — wind breaks, evaporation retarders — to keep surface and body in step.
  • Apply dry-shake toppings only at the right stage, never onto bleed water.
  • Sound-test suspect areas early so remediation is scoped before the floor goes into service.

None of these controls is new. What makes the difference is whether someone with no stake in the programme is watching the finishing window, and has the standing to raise a concern before the surface is closed. That is an oversight question as much as a technical one.

Key takeaways
  • Delamination is a finishing-window defect, not a strength defect.
  • It is caused by sealing the surface before bleed water and air have escaped.
  • Air entrainment, cold subgrades, membranes and rapid surface drying are major risk multipliers.
  • Sounding detects it; distinguishing it from curling or blistering decides the right remedy.
  • Reading the slab — not the clock — at the pour is the single best control.

What a professional snagging inspection should include — and what most miss

Handover is the point of greatest leverage on a new-build unit, and the point at which most snagging is weakest. What a proper inspection covers, the standards it should be measured against, and the gaps that let defects slip through.

A
Dr Arasti Afrasiabi
30 October 2026

Every new-build residential or commercial unit is handed over with some outstanding work. That is normal. What is not inevitable is how much of it survives into occupation, how long it takes to resolve, and who ends up paying for it. Those outcomes are decided less by the quality of the build than by the quality of the inspection carried out before the keys change hands.

For developers, contractors, housing associations and asset managers, a snagging inspection is the last structured opportunity to hold the works to the standard that was specified. Done well, it produces an evidence base that the contractor can act on directly. Done poorly, it produces a list that is argued over for months.

Snag or defect: why the distinction matters

The terms are often used interchangeably, but they describe different things. A snag is typically a cosmetic or finishing issue: a scuffed door, a missed patch of paint, an untidy sealant bead, a cracked tile. It is unsightly and should be put right, but it rarely affects performance.

A defect is a departure from what the works were required to be — non-compliance with the approved drawings, the specification, the relevant warranty provider’s technical standards or the Building Regulations. A missing cavity barrier, an incorrectly falling shower floor or an inadequately fixed balustrade is not a snag. It is a defect, and it may carry consequences for safety, durability and warranty cover.

The distinction matters because it shapes who is responsible, how urgently the item must be addressed and what evidence is needed to support the claim. An inspection that records everything as a generic snag makes it easier for genuine defects to be treated as cosmetic — and harder to insist they are corrected before handover.

When to inspect

There are two broad windows. The first is before handover or legal completion, while the contractor is still on site and the unit has not yet been accepted. The second is after completion, during the builder’s defects period — commonly the first two years under most new-home warranty schemes, though the precise terms depend on the warranty and the contract in place.

A post-completion inspection still has value, particularly to capture issues that only appear once a building is occupied and heated. But pre-handover inspection gives the client the most leverage. Labour and access are still available, remedial work can be programmed before occupation, and acceptance has not yet been given. Once a unit is accepted and occupied, every item becomes slower and more contested to resolve.

The best time to find a defect is before anyone has signed to say the unit is complete.

What a proper inspection covers

A professional snagging inspection is systematic, not a walk-round. Every room and every external element is inspected in a consistent sequence, so that nothing depends on what happens to catch the inspector’s eye. As a minimum, the scope should include:

  • Internal finishes room by room — walls, ceilings, floors, skirtings and decoration.
  • Doors and windows — operation, alignment, ironmongery, seals, glazing and trickle vents.
  • Joinery, kitchens and fitted furniture — fixing, alignment, damage and finish.
  • Sealants and tiling — continuity, falls to wet areas, grouting and cracked or hollow tiles.
  • Visible M&E terminations — sockets, switches, extract grilles, radiators and sanitaryware.
  • External envelope — brickwork, render, cladding interfaces, pointing, flashings and weep vents.
  • Drainage and external works — gullies, gradients, paving and boundary treatments.
  • Roofs and roof spaces where they can be safely accessed — coverings, ventilation and insulation.
  • Common areas — circulation spaces, stairs, lift lobbies, bin and cycle stores.
  • Fire-stopping and compartmentation visible on inspection — penetrations, fire doors and closers.

A visual inspection cannot verify concealed work, and a competent report says so plainly. What it can do is record precisely what is visible, flag where visible evidence suggests a concealed problem, and recommend further investigation where it is warranted.

The reference points

A snagging item carries weight when it is tied to a requirement. The inspection should be measured against the approved drawings and specification for the project; NHBC Standards, or the technical standards of whichever warranty provider applies; the Approved Documents supporting the Building Regulations; and, for new homes where it applies, the New Homes Quality Code. Where the specification is more onerous than the general standard, the specification governs.

None of these documents makes an inspection report legally decisive on its own, and a responsible inspector does not claim otherwise. Their value is that they convert an opinion into a referenced observation that the contractor can check and respond to.

What most inspections miss

Most snagging reports are not wrong so much as incomplete. The same gaps recur, and each one weakens the report’s usefulness at the point it is most needed.

  • No severity rating, so cosmetic items and genuine defects carry equal weight.
  • Vague locations — “bedroom wall” rather than a defined room, elevation and position.
  • No photographs, leaving each item open to dispute about what was actually seen.
  • No reference to the drawing, specification clause or standard that has been breached.
  • No re-inspection, so items are closed on the contractor’s word rather than on evidence.
  • An inspector who is not independent of the builder, which undermines the report’s weight.

What a good report looks like

A good snagging report is designed to be actioned, not just read. Each item should stand on its own, so that it can be allocated, remedied and verified without further explanation. That means recording, for every item:

  • A unique reference number that stays with the item through to close-out.
  • A precise location — unit, room, elevation and position.
  • A clear photograph, annotated where needed.
  • The drawing, specification or standard reference the item is measured against.
  • A severity rating that separates cosmetic snags from defects.
  • The responsible party, and the current close-out status.

The report should be issued in a format the contractor can work from directly — a schedule that can be sorted by unit, trade or severity — rather than a narrative document that has to be re-keyed before anything happens on site.

Re-inspection and close-out

A snagging list is only as valuable as the verification that follows it. Once the contractor reports items as complete, each one should be re-inspected against the original record and either closed with photographic evidence or carried forward. This is the step most often skipped, and it is the one that turns a list of findings into an assured handover.

A snag is not closed when the contractor says it is. It is closed when someone independent has looked again.

Key takeaways
  • A snag is a finishing issue; a defect is non-compliance with drawings, specification or standards — record them differently.
  • Pre-handover inspection gives the most leverage, before acceptance and occupation.
  • Every item should carry a reference, location, photo, standard reference and severity rating.
  • Re-inspection is what verifies close-out — not the contractor’s confirmation.
  • Independence from the builder is what gives a snagging report its weight.

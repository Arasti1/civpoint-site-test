Common defects in industrial floor construction and how to prevent them

From curling at saw-cut joints to abrasion failure under traffic — the defect catalogue every warehouse floor inherits, and the controls that close each route.

A
Dr Arasti Afrasiabi
February 2026

Industrial floors fail in predictable ways. The same defects recur across warehouses, distribution centres and manufacturing units because they share the same drivers: ambitious tolerances, fast programmes and the unforgiving duty of materials-handling traffic. Knowing the catalogue is the first step to designing it out.

In the UK, the Concrete Society’s TR34 (4th edition) is the recognised guidance for the design and construction of concrete industrial ground floors, including their surface regularity, and BS 8204-2 addresses concrete wearing surfaces and their abrasion resistance. Most of the defects below are departures from requirements those documents, and the project specification, already set.

Joints and load transfer

Joints are the planned weakness of an industrial floor, and often its first point of failure. Where slab edges curl or load transfer is inadequate, the joint is left unsupported and prone to spalling under wheel load.

  • Manage drying and moisture to limit differential shrinkage.
  • Provide engineered load transfer across trafficked joints.
  • Align joint layout with the racking and traffic pattern.

Curling and warping

The two terms are sometimes used interchangeably. In common usage, curling describes the upward lift of slab edges and corners caused by the top of the slab drying and shrinking more than the base, while warping is often used for similar distortion driven by temperature differences through the slab’s depth. Whatever the term, the effect is the same: loss of support at edges and corners, rocking under wheel load and increased risk of cracking and joint damage. Moisture-driven curling is generally most pronounced in the months after construction and tends to be greater where the slab can dry from its top surface only.

Curling can be reduced by limiting drying shrinkage through mix design, by appropriate joint spacing, by effective curing and, where specified, by reinforcement or other design measures. Where it has occurred, the right response depends on its extent and whether it is still developing; grinding or grouting a slab that is still moving can simply move the problem.

Surface defects: dusting and delamination

Dusting and delamination are finishing and curing failures. Dusting sheds a weak top layer under traffic; delamination separates a sealed crust from the slab beneath. Both are preventable by controlling site water, finishing timing and curing.

Crazing and plastic cracking

Crazing is a network of fine, shallow surface cracks, usually cosmetic, associated with rapid surface drying, over-trowelling or a paste-rich surface. Plastic shrinkage cracking forms in the first hours, when the surface loses moisture faster than bleeding replaces it, and is more likely in wind, low humidity and warm conditions. Both are reduced by protecting the slab from drying during and immediately after finishing, and by starting curing promptly.

Surface hardeners and dry-shake toppings

Dry-shake hardeners are widely used to improve abrasion resistance, but they are sensitive to application. Applied too early, onto bleed water or unevenly, they can contribute to delamination, colour variation or a weak surface layer. Application rate and timing should follow the manufacturer’s guidance and the specification, and should be checked during the pour rather than inferred afterwards.

Abrasion and flatness failures

Abrasion resistance failure shows as a surface that wears under repetitive trafficking — a function of mix, finishing and curing rather than bulk strength. Flatness non-compliance, meanwhile, is a tolerance failure that compromises MHE performance and, increasingly, automation.

Abrasion resistance is commonly specified by reference to the classes in BS 8204-2, which relate expected wear performance to the severity of use. The class should be chosen for the actual traffic — wheel type, loading and frequency — rather than defaulted.

Surface regularity is addressed by TR34, which distinguishes free-movement (FM) floors, where materials-handling equipment can travel in any direction, from defined-movement (DM) floors, where it runs in fixed aisles, as with very narrow aisle racking. Each family has classes of increasing stringency. The class should be fixed early, from the racking and MHE strategy, because it influences construction method, programme and cost.

MHE and racking interaction

Many floor problems only appear once racking and equipment arrive. Racking baseplates impose high point loads; joints beneath racking legs or across aisles affect both the slab and the equipment; and the tolerances required by high-lift or automated equipment may be more demanding than a general floor class. Coordinating joint layout, floor class and racking layout at design stage avoids conflicts that are difficult to resolve once the slab is cast.

Where the equipment supplier has specific floor requirements, these should be obtained before the floor is designed, not after it is built. Manufacturer limits may differ from the general floor class, and where they are more onerous they should be written into the specification.

An industrial floor does not get the benefit of the doubt. Whatever the pour inspection misses, the first year of traffic tends to find.

Inspection and testing before handover

Before handover — and before racking is installed — the floor should be checked against its specification while it is still accessible. Typical activities include:

  • Visual inspection for cracking, crazing, curling, joint damage and surface defects, with locations and photographs recorded.
  • Sounding of suspect areas for delamination or hollowness.
  • Checking joint widths, arris condition and joint filler.
  • Verification of surface regularity against the specified TR34 class by a suitably independent specialist.
  • Review of construction records, including pour, curing and hardener application records.

Defects found at this stage can be recorded, allocated and remedied while access is unobstructed. Once racking is installed, the same work becomes slower, more disruptive and more expensive.

Closing the routes to failure

  • Specify to the real operational duty and surface class, not a generic figure.
  • Treat finishing, curing and water control as the workmanship fundamentals.
  • Verify surface regularity against the specified class before racking and automation are installed.
  • Use independent QA/QC hold-points to catch defects at the pour, not after handover.
  • Coordinate joint layout, floor class and racking layout at design stage.

Key takeaways
  • Industrial floors fail in a predictable, recurring set of ways.
  • Curling, dusting, delamination, abrasion and flatness cover most of it.
  • Finishing, curing and water control are the shared workmanship fundamentals.
  • Fix the TR34 floor class and abrasion class early, from the real MHE and racking strategy.
  • Early verification of surface regularity and independent hold-points close the routes to failure.
